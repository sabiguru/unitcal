## The Core Principle: Mole Ratios

Stoichiometry uses balanced equations to convert between amounts of reactants and products.

**The fundamental approach:**
1. Balance the equation (coefficients show mole ratios)
2. Convert masses to moles
3. Use mole ratios to find product moles
4. Convert moles to desired units

**Mole conversions:**
- Moles = Mass (g) / Molar Mass (g/mol)
- Mass = Moles × Molar Mass

**Limiting reagent:**
The reactant that runs out first, determining maximum product formation. Other reactants are "in excess."

**Theoretical versus actual yield:**
- Theoretical: Maximum possible product (from stoichiometry)
- Actual: What you actually obtain (from experiment)
- Percent yield = (Actual / Theoretical) × 100

## How to Perform Stoichiometry Calculations

**Example: Hydrogen and oxygen forming water**
Equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Given: 10.0 g H₂ and 64.0 g O₂

**Step 1: Convert to moles**
H₂: 10.0 g / 2.016 g/mol = 4.96 mol
O₂: 64.0 g / 32.00 g/mol = 2.00 mol

**Step 2: Find limiting reagent**
From equation: 2 mol H₂ requires 1 mol O₂
Available: 4.96 mol H₂ would need 2.48 mol O₂
Available: 2.00 mol O₂ can react with 4.00 mol H₂

O₂ limits: only enough for 4.00 mol H₂ to react
→ O₂ is the limiting reagent

**Step 3: Calculate theoretical yield**
From equation: 1 mol O₂ → 2 mol H₂O
2.00 mol O₂ → 4.00 mol H₂O

Mass = 4.00 mol × 18.02 g/mol = 72.1 g H₂O

**Step 4: If actual yield was 65.0 g**
Percent yield = (65.0 / 72.1) × 100 = 90.2%

## Scenarios People Actually Run Into

**The unbalanced equation trap.** Unbalanced equations give wrong mole ratios. Always balance first.

**The limiting reagent confusion.** The reagent with fewer grams isn't necessarily limiting—molar amounts and ratios matter.

**The hydrate calculation.** CuSO₄·5H₂O has a different molar mass than anhydrous CuSO₄. Include water of crystallization.

**The gas volume conversion.** Gas volumes at RTP (24.0 L/mol for UK exams) must be converted to moles before applying stoichiometry.

**The percent yield over 100%.** If your actual yield exceeds theoretical, something is wrong—impurities, measurement error, or incomplete drying.

## Trade-Offs and Decisions People Underestimate

**Precision of molar masses.** Using 18 g/mol versus 18.015 g/mol for water affects calculations at high precision. Match precision to your needs.

**Excess reagent waste.** Using large excess ensures complete reaction but wastes expensive reagents. Balance economy against yield.

**Side reactions.** Real chemistry involves side reactions that reduce actual yield below theoretical.

**Purity assumptions.** Stoichiometry assumes 100% pure reagents. Impure reagents affect actual results.

**Significant figures.** Your answer shouldn't have more sig figs than your least precise measurement.

## Common Mistakes and How to Recover

**Forgetting to balance.** The calculator balances automatically, but understand why—coefficients are mole ratios.

**Using mass ratios instead of mole ratios.** You must convert to moles, apply the ratio, then convert back. Mass ratios only work if molar masses are equal.

**Identifying wrong limiting reagent.** Compare how much product each reactant could produce. The one producing less is limiting.

**Including spectator ions.** In ionic equations, spectator ions don't participate. Use net ionic equations when appropriate.

**Rounding intermediate steps.** Keep full precision through calculations; round only the final answer.

## Related Topics

**Molar mass.** Sum of atomic masses of all atoms in a formula.

**Avogadro's number.** 6.022 × 10²³ particles per mole.

**Limiting reagent.** The reactant that determines maximum product yield.

## How This Calculator Works

**Equation balancing:**
```
Parse chemical formulas
Count atoms of each element on both sides
Find smallest integer coefficients that balance
```

**Molar mass calculation:**
```
For each element in formula:
  mass += atomicMass × count
Return total mass
```

**Mole conversion:**
```
moles = mass / molarMass
```

**Limiting reagent identification:**
```
for each reactant:
  possibleProduct = (reactantMoles / coefficient) × productCoefficient
limitingReagent = reactant with minimum possibleProduct
```

**Theoretical yield:**
```
productMoles = (limitingMoles / limitingCoefficient) × productCoefficient
theoreticalYield = productMoles × productMolarMass
```

**Percent yield:**
```
percentYield = (actualYield / theoreticalYield) × 100
```

## FAQs

**Do I need to balance the equation myself?**

No. The calculator balances automatically. Just enter reactants and products correctly.

**Can I use this for ionic equations?**

Yes, as long as formulas are correct (e.g., AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3).

**How does it handle hydrates?**

Enter with a dot, like CuSO4·5H2O. The calculator includes water in molar mass calculations.

**What if I have only one reactant?**

The calculator assumes other reactants are in excess and calculates based on the given reactant.

**Does it work with gas volumes?**

Convert gas volume to moles first (using 24.0 L/mol at RTP), then input moles.

**Why does it show limiting reagent with two reactants?**

It only identifies limiting reagent when multiple reactants are provided. With one, excess is assumed.

**Can I calculate percent yield?**

Yes. Enter actual yield from your experiment, and the calculator computes percent yield.

**What molar masses does it use?**

Standard atomic masses from the periodic table, updated to current IUPAC values.
