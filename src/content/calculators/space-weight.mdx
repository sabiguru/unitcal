## The Core Principle: Gravity and Weight

Weight is the force of gravity acting on your mass. Your mass stays constant everywhere, but weight changes with local gravity.

**Weight formula:**
Weight_new = Weight_Earth × (g_new / g_Earth)

Where g is surface gravity. This can also be expressed as:
Weight_new = Weight_Earth × Gravity_Ratio

**Gravity depends on two factors:**
g ∝ Mass / Radius²

A body with more mass has stronger gravity. A body with larger radius has weaker surface gravity (you're farther from the center).

**Key gravity ratios (relative to Earth = 1.00):**
- Moon: 0.17
- Mars: 0.38
- Venus: 0.91
- Jupiter: 2.53
- Saturn: 1.07
- Pluto: 0.06

## How to Calculate Weight on Other Planets

**Example: Your weight on Mars**
Earth weight: 150 pounds
Mars gravity ratio: 0.38

Mars weight = 150 × 0.38 = 57 pounds

You'd weigh only 57 pounds on Mars—less than half your Earth weight!

**Example: Your weight on Jupiter**
Earth weight: 150 pounds
Jupiter gravity ratio: 2.53

Jupiter weight = 150 × 2.53 = 380 pounds

Jupiter's massive gravity would make you feel 2.5× heavier.

**Example: Your weight on the Moon**
Earth weight: 150 pounds
Moon gravity ratio: 0.17

Moon weight = 150 × 0.17 = 25.5 pounds

The Moon's weak gravity is why astronauts could bounce around during the Apollo missions.

## Scenarios People Actually Run Into

**The Saturn surprise.** Students expect massive Saturn to have crushing gravity, but its huge radius means surface gravity is only slightly more than Earth's (1.07×).

**The mass versus weight confusion.** "Do I weigh less on the Moon?" Yes. "Is my mass less?" No—mass is constant. This distinction is fundamental.

**The gas giant "surface."** Jupiter and Saturn have no solid surface. Their gravity is measured at the 1-bar atmospheric pressure level.

**The Pluto debate.** Pluto's tiny gravity (0.06×) makes it clear why it was reclassified—it's more like a large asteroid than a planet.

**The Sun calculation.** You could calculate Sun weight (28× Earth), but you couldn't stand on it—it's plasma with no surface.

## Trade-Offs and Decisions People Underestimate

**Habitability versus comfort.** Mars's low gravity (0.38×) might feel liberating initially but could cause long-term health issues (bone loss, muscle atrophy).

**Rocket fuel requirements.** Lower gravity means easier launches. This is why the Moon and Mars are attractive for space exploration staging.

**Atmospheric effects.** Gravity affects atmosphere retention. Mars's low gravity contributes to its thin atmosphere.

**Structural engineering.** Buildings, vehicles, and bodies evolved for Earth gravity. Different gravities require different designs.

**Adaptation timelines.** Human bodies adapt to different gravity levels, but the process takes time and has limits.

## Common Mistakes and How to Recover

**Confusing mass and weight.** Mass (kg) is constant everywhere. Weight (force) changes with gravity. Be precise with terminology.

**Using mass units for weight.** In everyday usage, "kg" often means weight (more precisely, kilogram-force). The calculator handles this common convention.

**Forgetting gas giants have no surface.** Jupiter and Saturn gravity values are for the 1-bar level—you couldn't actually stand there.

**Thinking gravity is absent in orbit.** Astronauts in the ISS experience microgravity because they're falling, not because gravity is absent. At ISS altitude, gravity is still ~90% of surface strength.

**Assuming linear scaling.** Gravity doesn't scale linearly with mass because radius also matters. Saturn is 95× Earth's mass but only 1.07× its surface gravity.

## Related Topics

**Mass versus weight.** Mass is the amount of matter; weight is gravitational force. The distinction is crucial for physics.

**Surface gravity.** Gravitational acceleration at a body's surface, measured in m/s² or relative to Earth (g).

**Escape velocity.** The speed needed to leave a body's gravitational pull, related to but distinct from surface gravity.

**Tidal forces.** Differential gravity across an object's extent, important near massive bodies.

**Microgravity.** The near-weightless conditions in orbit, caused by freefall rather than absence of gravity.

## FAQs

**What's the difference between mass and weight?**

Mass is the amount of matter (constant everywhere). Weight is gravitational force on that mass (varies with location). This calculator shows weight.

**Are the gravity values accurate?**

Yes. We use standard values from NASA and the International Astronomical Union. Gas giant values are at the 1-bar atmospheric level.

**Why is my weight on Saturn almost the same as Earth?**

Saturn is massive (95× Earth) but huge, diluting surface gravity. At the cloud-top "surface," gravity is only 1.07× Earth's.

**Can I use kilograms?**

Yes. If you enter 70 kg, the calculator treats it as Earth weight (like 70 kgf). Your mass would be ~70 kg; your Earth weight ~686 N.

**Does this work for the Sun?**

Technically yes (ratio 27.9×), but the Sun has no solid surface—it's plasma. The value is included for educational comparison.

**How is the Moon's gravity so low?**

The Moon has only 1.2% of Earth's mass and 27% of Earth's radius, resulting in about 1/6th Earth's gravity.

**Is there gravity in space?**

Yes. Astronauts in orbit experience microgravity because they're in freefall, not because gravity is absent.

**Can I calculate weight on exoplanets?**

Not in this tool, but if you know an exoplanet's mass (M) and radius (R), estimate gravity using g ∝ M/R².
