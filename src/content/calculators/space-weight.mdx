## Space Weight Calculator — Your Weight on Planets & Moons
Discover what you’d weigh on the Moon, Mars, Jupiter, and other celestial bodies with our Space Weight Calculator. This tool demonstrates a key physics principle: while your **mass** stays constant, your **weight** changes with gravity. Simply enter your Earth weight, and the calculator instantly shows your weight across the solar system using real NASA gravity data.

### How It Works
- **Core Formula**: `Weight_new = Weight_earth × (g_new / g_earth)`  
- **Gravity Ratios**: Pre-loaded with accurate surface gravity values for planets, moons, and dwarf planets.
- **Units**: Works with pounds (lb) or kilograms (kg)—interpreted as Earth weight (force).

### Why It Matters
- **Educational**: Perfect for students learning Newtonian physics and the mass/weight distinction.
- **Curiosity**: Satisfy your wonder about life on other worlds.

This calculator is ideal for classrooms, science fairs, and anyone fascinated by our solar system.

## Step-by-Step: Calculating Weight on Mars
**Scenario**: You weigh **150 pounds** on Earth. What would you weigh on Mars?

1. **Enter 150** as your Earth weight.
2. **Select Mars** from the celestial body list.
3. **The calculator applies Mars’ gravity ratio (0.38)**:
   - `150 lb × 0.38 = 57 lb`
4. **Result**: You would weigh **57 pounds** on Mars—less than half your Earth weight!

Try it with Jupiter (2.53×) to see how heavy you’d feel, or Pluto (0.06×) to feel nearly weightless!

### What’s the difference between mass and weight?
**Mass** is the amount of matter (constant everywhere). **Weight** is the force of gravity on that mass (changes with location). This calculator shows your *weight* on other worlds, not your mass.

### Are the gravity values accurate?
Yes. We use standard surface gravity ratios from NASA and the International Astronomical Union (IAU). For gas giants, gravity is given at the 1-bar atmospheric level.

### Why is my weight on Saturn almost the same as on Earth?
Saturn is massive (95× Earth), but its large radius dilutes surface gravity. At its cloud-top “surface,” gravity is only **1.07× Earth’s**.

### Can I use kilograms?
Yes—but note: if you enter **70 kg**, the calculator treats it as your *Earth weight* (like 70 kgf), not mass. Your mass would actually be ~70 kg, and your Earth weight ~686 N.

### Does this work for the Sun?
Technically yes (gravity ratio = 27.9), but you couldn’t stand on the Sun—it’s a plasma ball with no solid surface. The value is included for educational comparison.

### How is the Moon’s gravity so low?
The Moon has only **1.2% of Earth’s mass** and **27% of Earth’s radius**, resulting in **1/6th Earth’s gravity**.

### Is there gravity in space?
Yes! Astronauts in orbit experience **microgravity** because they’re in freefall around Earth—not because gravity is absent. At the ISS’s altitude, gravity is still ~90% of Earth’s surface strength.

### Can I calculate weight on exoplanets?
Not in this tool—but if you know an exoplanet’s mass and radius, you can estimate its gravity using `g ∝ M/R²`.

### Why do gas giants have “surface” gravity?
Since they lack a solid surface, scientists define “surface” gravity at the atmospheric pressure level of **1 bar** (equal to Earth’s sea-level pressure).

### Is this suitable for school projects?
Absolutely. It’s a safe, engaging way to teach Newton’s laws, gravity, and planetary science. Try comparing weights across all planets to see the solar system’s diversity!
