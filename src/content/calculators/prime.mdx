## How to Use the Prime Number Calculator
The **prime number calculator** performs two essential number theory tasks: **checking if a number is prime** and **finding its prime factorisation**. Simply enter any integer greater than 1, and the tool instantly determines its primality using efficient algorithms. For composite numbers, it breaks them down into a product of prime factors in exponential form. It can also **generate a list of all prime numbers up to a specified limit** using the Sieve of Eratosthenes. This makes it perfect for homework verification, cryptographic exploration, or satisfying mathematical curiosity.

### Key Functions & Methods
- **Primality Test**: Uses trial division for small numbers and optimised algorithms for larger ones.
- **Prime Factorisation**: Applies repeated division by smallest primes to build a complete factor tree.
- **Prime Generation**: Implements the Sieve of Eratosthenes for fast, accurate prime lists.
- **Fundamental Theorem Compliance**: Ensures every factorisation is unique and complete.

### Best Practices for Effective Use
1.  **For primality testing**, enter numbers up to 10¹² for instant results.
2.  **For factorisation**, the calculator handles numbers up to 10¹⁵ efficiently.
3.  **When generating primes**, limits up to 100,000 are processed in seconds.
4.  **Use the results to cross-verify manual calculations**—especially for exams or homework.
5.  **Explore patterns**: Generate primes in ranges to study gaps, twins, or distributions.

### How does the calculator test for primality?
For numbers **below 10⁶**, it uses **trial division up to √n**. For larger numbers, it employs a combination of **deterministic checks** (for small factors) and the **Miller-Rabin probabilistic test** with multiple bases, which is >99.999% accurate for practical purposes.

### Why does factorisation stop at prime numbers?
Because of the **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic**: every integer >1 has a unique representation as a product of primes. Once all factors are prime, the decomposition is complete and cannot be simplified further.

### Can this calculator handle very large numbers?
It can factorise numbers up to **10¹⁵** reliably. Beyond that, factorisation becomes computationally intensive—even for supercomputers. This is the basis of modern RSA encryption, which uses primes with hundreds of digits.

### What is the largest number I can test for primality?
The primality test works for integers up to **10¹⁸**. For numbers in this range, the Miller-Rabin test provides a **probable prime** result with extremely high confidence.

### Is 2 really a prime number?
Yes. **2 is the only even prime** because it has exactly two divisors: 1 and 2. All other even numbers are divisible by 2 and hence composite.

### Why does the Sieve of Eratosthenes stop at √n?
Because any composite number ≤ n must have a prime factor ≤ √n. Crossing out multiples beyond √n is redundant—all composites would have already been marked by smaller primes.

### Can I use this for homework?
Absolutely. The calculator shows the **full factor tree** and **primality logic**, making it an excellent tool for learning and verifying your manual work. Just ensure you understand the steps—it’s a tutor, not a shortcut.

### What if I enter 1 or 0?
The calculator will return an error or note that **1 and 0 are not prime** by definition. Prime numbers must be **natural numbers greater than 1**.
