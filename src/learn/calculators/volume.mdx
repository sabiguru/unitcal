## Core Volume Formulas: From Theory to Practice
Every 3D shape has a specific formula derived from its geometry. The key is to identify the shape and apply the correct equation.

### 1. Rectangular Prism (Cuboid)
The most common shape for boxes, rooms, and storage containers.
```
V = length × width × height
```
**Example**: A shipping box is 60 cm × 40 cm × 30 cm.  
`V = 60 × 40 × 30 = 72,000 cm³ = 72 L`

### 2. Cylinder
Used for pipes, tanks, cans, and columns.
```
V = π r² h
```
- `r` = radius (half the diameter)
- `h` = height (or length)

**Critical Tip**: Always use **radius**, not diameter.  
**Example**: A water tank has a diameter of 2 m and height of 3 m.  
`r = 1 m`, so `V = π × 1² × 3 ≈ 9.42 m³ = 9,420 L`

### 3. Sphere
Applies to balls, domes, and spherical tanks.
```
V = (4/3) × π × r³
```
**Example**: A football has a radius of 11 cm.  
`V = (4/3) × π × 11³ ≈ 5,575 cm³`

### 4. Cone
Used for funnels, traffic cones, and conical piles.
```
V = (1/3) × π × r² × h
```
Note the `1/3` factor—cones hold one-third the volume of a cylinder with the same base and height.

### 5. Ellipsoid
A 3D ellipse, useful for modelling tanks or biological cells.
```
V = (4/3) × π × a × b × c
```
- `a, b, c` = semi-axes (half the lengths in each dimension)

### Unit Conversions: Avoiding Costly Mistakes
Volume units are **cubed**, so conversion factors are also cubed:
- `1 m³ = 1,000 L`
- `1 L = 1,000 cm³`
- `1 ft³ ≈ 28.317 L`
- `1 US gal = 3.785 L`

**Pro Tip**: Convert all dimensions to the same unit **before** calculating volume.

## Calculating Partial Volumes: Real-World Applications
Many practical problems involve **partially filled containers**, especially horizontal cylindrical tanks (e.g., fuel or propane tanks).

### Horizontal Cylindrical Tank
The volume of liquid depends on the **fill height**. The formula involves the **circular segment area**:
```
V = L × [r² × cos⁻¹((r - h)/r) - (r - h) × √(2rh - h²)]
```
- `L` = tank length
- `r` = radius
- `h` = fill height

**Simplified Approach**: For quick estimates:
- **50% full** → half the total volume
- **25% full** → ~20% of total volume (due to curved bottom)

### Composite Shapes
Break complex objects into simpler parts:
**Example**: A silo = cylinder + hemisphere on top.  
Calculate each volume separately and add them.

## Pro Tips for Accuracy and Efficiency
- **Measure Internally**: For containers, use internal dimensions to find usable volume.
- **Account for Wall Thickness**: A 1 m³ external tank may hold only 0.95 m³ internally.
- **Use Consistent Precision**: Carry extra digits in intermediate steps; round only the final answer.
- **Verify Shape Assumptions**: Real-world objects are rarely perfect geometric shapes—allow a 5–10% margin for error.
- **Leverage Technology**: Use a laser measure for large spaces (e.g., rooms) to improve accuracy.

## Worked Examples & Scenario Planning

### Example 1: Aquarium Water Volume
**Problem**: A rectangular aquarium is 120 cm long, 50 cm wide, and 60 cm high. How many litres of water does it hold when filled to 55 cm?

**Solution**:
- Use fill height: 55 cm
- `V = 120 × 50 × 55 = 330,000 cm³`
- Convert: `330,000 ÷ 1,000 = 330 L`

✅ **Answer**: **330 litres**

### Example 2: Concrete for Cylindrical Footings
**Problem**: You need to pour 8 cylindrical footings, each 40 cm in diameter and 1.2 m deep. How much concrete (in m³) is required?

**Solution**:
- Radius = 20 cm = 0.2 m
- Volume per footing: `π × (0.2)² × 1.2 ≈ 0.1508 m³`
- Total: `0.1508 × 8 = 1.206 m³`
- Add 10% waste: `1.206 × 1.10 ≈ 1.33 m³`

✅ **Answer**: Order **1.33 cubic metres** of concrete.

### Example 3: Partially Filled Horizontal Tank
**Problem**: A propane tank is 2 m long with a 1 m diameter. The gauge shows a fill height of 30 cm. What’s the volume of propane?

**Solution**:
- Radius = 0.5 m, fill height = 0.3 m
- Use segment formula or approximation:
  - Fill ratio = `0.3 / 1.0 = 30%`
  - Approximate volume = 25% of total
- Total tank volume: `π × (0.5)² × 2 ≈ 1.57 m³`
- Propane volume ≈ `1.57 × 0.25 = 0.39 m³ = 390 L`

### Practice Problems (Try These!)
1. A sphere has a diameter of 20 cm. What’s its volume in cm³?
2. A room is 5 m × 4 m × 2.5 m. What’s its volume in m³ and ft³?
3. A conical pile of sand has a base diameter of 3 m and height of 1.5 m. What’s its volume?

**Answers**:  
1. `(4/3) × π × 10³ ≈ 4,189 cm³`  
2. `50 m³`; `50 ÷ 0.028317 ≈ 1,766 ft³`  
3. `(1/3) × π × (1.5)² × 1.5 ≈ 3.53 m³`

### What’s the difference between volume and capacity?
- **Volume**: Total 3D space an object occupies (e.g., a brick’s volume).
- **Capacity**: Amount of fluid a container can hold (e.g., a bottle’s capacity).  
In practice, for hollow containers, capacity = internal volume.

### How do I calculate the volume of an irregular shape?
Use the **displacement method**: submerge the object in water and measure the volume of water displaced. For digital modelling, break the shape into small regular elements (voxels) and sum their volumes.

### Why is my calculated tank volume larger than the manufacturer’s rating?
Manufacturers list **usable capacity**, which excludes space taken by internal fittings, domed ends, or safety margins. Your geometric calculation gives **total internal volume**—expect a 5–10% difference.

### Can I use diameter instead of radius in the cylinder formula?
No. The formula `π r² h` requires radius. If you have diameter `d`, use `r = d/2`, so the formula becomes `π (d/2)² h = (π d² h)/4`.

### How do I convert cubic metres to litres?
Multiply by 1,000: `1 m³ = 1,000 L`. This is because 1 litre = 1,000 cm³, and 1 m³ = 1,000,000 cm³.

### What if my room isn’t a perfect rectangle?
Divide it into rectangular sections (e.g., main area + alcove), calculate each volume, and sum them. For sloped ceilings, use the average height.

### How accurate are online volume calculators?
They’re highly accurate for ideal shapes. For real-world applications, always add a **waste factor** (5–10%) to account for measurement error, material settling, or imperfect geometry.

### Is there a formula for a pyramid?
Yes: `V = (1/3) × base area × height`. A square pyramid with 2 m base and 3 m height has `V = (1/3) × 4 × 3 = 4 m³`.

### Why do horizontal tanks need special formulas?
Because the cross-sectional area of liquid changes non-linearly with height—the bottom is curved, so 30% height ≠ 30% volume.
