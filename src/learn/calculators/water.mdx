## The Science of Hydration: Why One Size Doesn’t Fit All
Your body is approximately **60% water**, and every cell, tissue, and organ relies on it to function. Water regulates temperature, lubricates joints, transports nutrients, and flushes waste. Because we lose water continuously through breathing, sweating, and urination, we must replenish it daily—but the amount varies significantly from person to person.

### Core Calculation Methods
#### 1. Weight-Based Formula (Most Accurate for Baseline)
The most widely accepted method uses your body weight to establish a baseline:
- **Metric**: `Water (litres) = Body weight (kg) × 0.033`
- **Imperial**: `Water (oz) = Body weight (lbs) × 0.67`

**Example**:  
- A 70 kg adult: `70 × 0.033 = 2.31 L/day`  
- A 154 lb adult: `154 × 0.67 ≈ 103 oz/day` (≈ 3.05 L)

This formula aligns with guidelines from the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), which recommends **2.0 L for women** and **2.5 L for men** from fluids alone (total water includes food).

#### 2. Activity Adjustment
Exercise increases fluid loss through sweat. Add **500–1000 mL (16–32 oz)** for every hour of moderate activity. For intense or prolonged exercise, consider an electrolyte drink to replace sodium and potassium.

#### 3. Climate and Environment
- **Hot/humid weather**: Add **500–750 mL (16–25 oz)**
- **High altitude (>2,500 m)**: Add **500 mL (16 oz)** due to increased respiratory water loss
- **Heated/cooled indoor air**: Dry air increases insensible water loss—add **250–500 mL (8–16 oz)**

### Understanding Total Water vs. Fluid Intake
- **Total water**: Includes water from **all beverages and food** (e.g., fruits, vegetables, soups contribute ~20% of intake)
- **Fluid intake**: Refers only to **drinks** (water, tea, milk, etc.)
Most guidelines (like EFSA’s) specify fluid intake, so focus on drinks unless otherwise noted.

### Recognising Hydration Status
#### Signs of Dehydration
- Thirst (a late indicator)
- Dark yellow urine (aim for pale straw colour)
- Fatigue, headache, dizziness
- Dry mouth, reduced skin elasticity

#### Signs of Overhydration (Hyponatremia)
- Nausea, headache, confusion
- Swelling in hands/feet
- Clear, frequent urination
Rare but dangerous—usually occurs with excessive water intake (>1 L/hour for several hours).

### Practical Hydration Strategies
- **Start your day with water**: Drink 500 mL upon waking to rehydrate after sleep.
- **Use a marked water bottle**: Choose a 750 mL or 1 L bottle and aim to finish 2–3 per day.
- **Pair water with habits**: Drink a glass before each meal, after bathroom breaks, or during work breaks.
- **Flavour naturally**: Add lemon, cucumber, or mint if plain water is unappealing.
- **Monitor urine colour**: Use a hydration chart—pale yellow = well-hydrated.

### Example 1: Sedentary Office Worker
**Profile**:

- No regular exercise

**Calculation**:  
- Baseline: `60 kg × 0.033 = 1.98 L`  
- No activity/climate adjustments  
- **Target**: **2.0 L of fluids/day**

### Example 2: Active Male in Hot Climate
**Profile**:  
- Male, 85 kg, lives in Dubai, cycles 1 hour daily  
- Average temperature: 35°C

**Calculation**:  
- Baseline: `85 × 0.033 = 2.81 L`  
- Exercise: +1.0 L  
- Climate: +0.75 L  
- **Target**: **4.56 L of fluids/day**

### Example 3: Pregnant Woman
**Profile**:  
- Female, 68 kg, 28 weeks pregnant, light activity  
- Lives in Manchester

**Calculation**:  
- Baseline: `68 × 0.033 = 2.24 L`  
- Pregnancy adds ~300 mL (NHS guidance)  
- **Target**: **2.5 L of fluids/day**

### Is the “8 glasses a day” rule accurate?
It’s a useful mnemonic but oversimplified. Eight 8-oz glasses = 64 oz (1.9 L), which is close to the average woman’s needs but may be too little for men or active individuals. Use it as a minimum, not a universal target.

### Do tea and coffee count toward hydration?
Yes. Despite mild diuretic effects, the water content in tea and coffee still contributes to net fluid balance. However, water remains the optimal choice for primary hydration.

### How does alcohol affect hydration?
Alcohol is a potent diuretic—it increases urine production and impairs water reabsorption. For every alcoholic drink, consume an equal amount of water to offset dehydration.

### Should I drink more water if I’m breastfeeding?
Yes. The NHS recommends breastfeeding women drink **~2.7 L of fluids daily** to support milk production and maternal health.

### Can I rely on thirst to guide me?
Thirst is a reliable indicator for most healthy adults, but it lags behind actual need. By the time you feel thirsty, you’re already mildly dehydrated. Older adults and athletes should not rely solely on thirst.

### What about electrolyte drinks?
For exercise below 1 hour, water is sufficient. For longer/intense sessions, electrolyte drinks help replace sodium lost in sweat and improve fluid retention.

### Does water intake affect weight loss?
Yes. Drinking water before meals can promote satiety, and mild dehydration can slow metabolism. Replacing sugary drinks with water is one of the most effective dietary changes for weight management.

### How much water is too much?
The kidneys can excrete ~0.8–1.0 L/hour. Drinking >1 L/hour for several hours can cause **hyponatremia** (dangerously low blood sodium). Listen to your body—clear, frequent urination is a sign to ease up.

### Are there medical conditions that require fluid restriction?
Yes. Heart failure, kidney disease, and certain medications (e.g., diuretics) may require controlled fluid intake. Always follow your doctor’s advice in these cases.

### What’s the best type of water to drink?
Tap water (in the UK) is safe and regulated. Filtered, bottled, or sparkling water are fine if preferred. The key is consistency—not the source.
