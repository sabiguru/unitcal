## Mass vs. Weight: The Core Concept
Before calculating, it’s essential to distinguish these two often-confused terms:

- **Mass** is the amount of matter in your body, measured in **kilograms (kg)** or **pounds-mass (lbm)**. It’s an intrinsic property and **does not change** with location.
- **Weight** is the **force** exerted on your mass by gravity, measured in **Newtons (N)** or **pounds-force (lbf)**. It **varies** with gravitational strength.

On Earth, we colloquially say “I weigh 70 kg,” but technically, 70 kg is our *mass*. Our *weight* is approximately **686 N** (70 kg × 9.8 m/s²).

## The Physics Formula: Newton’s Second Law
Weight is calculated using **Newton’s second law**:

**`Weight = Mass × Gravitational Acceleration`**  
or  
**`W = m × g`**

Where:
- `W` = weight (in N or lbf)  
- `m` = mass (in kg or lbm)  
- `g` = surface gravity (in m/s² or ft/s²)

### Step-by-Step Calculation Process
Since most people know their **Earth weight** (not mass), we use a two-step method:

1. **Find your mass**:  
   `m = W_earth / g_earth`  
   (On Earth, `g_earth ≈ 9.8 m/s²` or `32.2 ft/s²`)

2. **Calculate weight on another body**:  
   `W_new = m × g_new`

This simplifies to a single ratio formula:

**`W_new = W_earth × (g_new / g_earth)`**

The term `(g_new / g_earth)` is the **gravity ratio**—a dimensionless number that tells you what fraction of your Earth weight you’d experience elsewhere.

> **Note**: For gas giants (Jupiter, Saturn, etc.), “surface” gravity is defined at the 1-bar pressure level in their atmospheres, as they lack a solid surface.

## Why Gravity Differs: Mass and Radius
Surface gravity depends on two factors:
- **Planetary mass (M)**: More mass = stronger gravity.
- **Distance from centre (R)**: Larger radius = weaker gravity at the surface.

The formula is:  
**`g = G × M / R²`**  
(where `G` is the gravitational constant)

This explains why **Saturn** (95× Earth’s mass) has only **1.07× Earth’s gravity**—its enormous radius (9.5× Earth’s) dilutes the gravitational pull at its “surface.”

## Practical Implications of Variable Gravity
- **Moon (0.17g)**: Astronauts can leap 6× higher and carry heavy gear with ease—ideal for exploration but requiring adaptation.
- **Mars (0.38g)**: Long-term exposure causes muscle atrophy and bone loss; future habitats may need artificial gravity.
- **Jupiter (2.53g)**: A 70 kg person would “weigh” **177 kg**—movement would be extremely difficult, and the body would struggle to circulate blood.
- **Venus (0.91g)**: Gravity is Earth-like, but surface conditions (460°C, 90 atm pressure) make survival impossible.

## Common Misconceptions Clarified
❌ **“There’s no gravity in space.”**  
✅ Gravity exists everywhere! Astronauts in orbit experience **microgravity** because they’re in freefall—not because gravity is absent.

❌ **“Heavier planets always have stronger surface gravity.”**  
✅ Not necessarily. **Density and radius** matter just as much. Uranus is 14× Earth’s mass but has *less* surface gravity due to its low density and large size.

❌ **“My mass changes on other planets.”**  
✅ No—only your *weight* (the force) changes. Your mass is constant.

## Pro Tips for Accurate Calculations
✅ **Use consistent units**: If your Earth weight is in pounds, your space weight will be in pounds-force.  
✅ **Round reasonably**: Gravity ratios are averages; use 2–3 significant figures for realistic estimates.  
✅ **Consider rotation**: Fast-spinning planets (e.g., Jupiter) have slightly lower gravity at the equator due to centrifugal force—but this effect is minor for basic calculations.  
✅ **For moons and dwarf planets**, use authoritative sources like NASA for gravity values.

## Worked Examples & Practice Problems

### Example 1: Weight on the Moon
**Given**: Earth weight = **150 lb**  
**Gravity ratio (Moon)** = 0.17  
**Calculation**:  
`W_moon = 150 × 0.17 = 25.5 lb`  
**Interpretation**: You’d feel as light as a 25-pound child on Earth.

### Example 2: Weight on Jupiter
**Given**: Earth weight = **70 kg** (mass)  
**Step 1**: Find Earth weight in Newtons:  
`W_earth = 70 × 9.8 = 686 N`  
**Step 2**: Apply Jupiter’s ratio (2.53):  
`W_jupiter = 686 × 2.53 ≈ 1,736 N`  
**Alternative**: Directly use mass:  
`W_jupiter = 70 × 24.8 = 1,736 N`  
**In pounds**: 1,736 N ÷ 4.45 ≈ **390 lb**

### Example 3: Weight on Pluto
**Given**: Earth weight = **120 lb**  
**Gravity ratio (Pluto)** = 0.06  
**Calculation**:  
`W_pluto = 120 × 0.06 = 7.2 lb`  
You’d weigh less than a bag of sugar!

### Practice Problems
1. A person weighs **80 kg** on Earth. What is their weight on **Mars** in Newtons?  
2. If you weigh **160 lb** on Earth, what is your weight on **Venus**?  
3. Calculate your own weight on **Neptune** and **Mercury**.  
4. Why does **Saturn** have nearly Earth-like surface gravity despite being much more massive?

### Answers
1. `80 × 3.7 = 296 N`  
2. `160 × 0.91 = 145.6 lb`  
3. Use ratios: Neptune = ×1.14, Mercury = ×0.38  
4. Saturn’s large radius (R² in denominator) offsets its high mass.

### Challenge: Convert to Kilograms-Force
In some contexts, “kg” is used as a force unit (kgf), where 1 kgf = 9.8 N.  
If your Earth “weight” is **70 kgf**, your Moon weight is **70 × 0.17 = 11.9 kgf**—but your *mass* is still **70 kg**.

### How do scientists measure gravity on other planets?
Gravity is determined through:
- **Orbital mechanics**: Tracking how a planet affects spacecraft or moons (using Kepler’s laws and Newtonian gravity).
- **Direct measurement**: Landers like *InSight* on Mars use accelerometers to measure local `g`.
- **Mathematical modeling**: Using known mass and radius in `g = GM/R²`.

### Would I float on Saturn?
No. Saturn’s “surface” (1-bar level) has **1.07× Earth’s gravity**, so you’d feel slightly heavier. However, Saturn is a gas giant—you’d sink into increasingly dense layers, not float on a surface.

### Can I calculate weight on exoplanets?
Yes—if you know the exoplanet’s **mass and radius**, you can estimate `g` using `g ∝ M/R²`. Many exoplanet databases (e.g., NASA Exoplanet Archive) provide these values.

### Why is the Moon’s gravity 1/6th of Earth’s?
The Moon has **1.2% of Earth’s mass** and **27% of Earth’s radius**. Plugging into `g = GM/R²`:  
`(0.012) / (0.27)² ≈ 0.012 / 0.073 ≈ 0.16` → ~1/6.

### Does altitude affect weight on Earth?
Yes—but minimally. At the top of Mount Everest (~8,850 m), gravity is about **0.3% weaker** than at sea level. You’d weigh ~0.2 lb less if you weigh 150 lb.

### How does gravity affect time (relativity)?
According to Einstein’s general relativity, **time runs slower in stronger gravitational fields**. On Jupiter, your clock would tick slightly slower than on Earth—but the difference is nanoseconds per year, not noticeable without atomic clocks.

### Could humans live on a high-gravity planet?
Long-term exposure to >1.5g would likely cause cardiovascular strain, muscle fatigue, and skeletal stress. Current human limits are unknown, but sustained >2g is probably unsustainable without advanced technology.

### What about black holes?
Near a black hole, gravity is so intense that not even light escapes. Your “weight” would approach infinity as you neared the event horizon—but you’d be torn apart by tidal forces long before.

### Is there a planet with zero gravity?
No. Every object with mass exerts gravity. Even asteroids have measurable (though tiny) gravity—enough to allow spacecraft to orbit them.

### How accurate are these gravity ratios?
They’re based on NASA and IAU data and are accurate to within **1–2%** for major bodies. Smaller moons and irregular objects have less precise values.
